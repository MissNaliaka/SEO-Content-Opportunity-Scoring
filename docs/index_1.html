01 · Abstract

Abstract

Which pages should a content team review first — for refresh, expansion, protection, pruning, or monitoring? This study builds a ranked priority-scoring system on 260,285 client–page rows from the FlyRank ML Internship data warehouse, covering a 90-day feature window (2026-01-30 to 2026-04-30) across 41 real clients. A rule-based baseline and three learned models (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest) were compared under a size-balanced, client-grouped validation split, against a genuinely forward-looking label — whether a page's impressions were still declining in May 2026. The decision tree scored highest at every K (Precision@50 = 0.940 vs. the baseline's 0.62), but a permutation-importance audit found this apparent win rested almost entirely on one feature tied mechanically to how the label was built, not a real content-quality signal — a finding this paper reports in full rather than hiding. The result is a ranked review queue with reason codes, intended as decision-support for a human reviewer, not an automated or causal claim about what makes content succeed.

02 · Introduction

Introduction & the problem

FlyRank's content teams manage far more pages than any reviewer can manually audit in a week. Traditional analytics — visits, time on page, conversions — describe what happened, but not which of hundreds of underperforming pages is worth an hour of a reviewer's time today. Treating every "declining" page the same wastes that hour: a reviewer sent to refresh a page that should have been pruned, or to monitor one that urgently needs a rewrite, is a wrong recommendation with a real cost.

This is the concrete case study behind this paper. Content decay in search is not a single signal — a page can lose impressions because it genuinely fell out of date, because a competitor out-ranked it, because a SERP feature started absorbing clicks that never reached the page at all, or because of a technical/indexing issue that has nothing to do with the content itself. A single if-statement threshold cannot hold all of that at once. This project frames the FlyRank review problem as a ranking task: given a page's observed search behavior over a trailing window, produce a score and a reason code that tells a human reviewer where to look first — and, just as importantly, when the score should not be trusted.

03 · Data

Data

Source: the FlyRank ML Internship Hugging Face warehouse (dim_content, dim_clients, fact_content_daily_performance) — real production search data at far larger scale than the bundled anonymized starter sample.

Decision moment: 2026-04-30. Feature window: 2026-01-30 to 2026-04-30 (90 days). Check-only label window: May 2026, used only to check whether decline continued after the decision moment — never as a model feature.

Qualifying population: 260,285 client–page rows across 41 clients, requiring a full 90-day history (no partial windows silently padded with zeros).

Excluded, and why:

  • content_updated_date, last_optimized_date — snapshot-table fields found to be systematically future-dated relative to a page's real edit history; using them as a staleness signal risked leaking post-decision information.
  • optimization_eligible_date, is_published, is_deleted — product/workflow state, not a search-behavior signal.
  • Any performance row after 2026-04-30 — used only to build the check-only label, never as a feature.
  • Per-platform AI-referral session columns — too sparse per page to be reliable, and outside the feature budget.

Public-safe by construction: no client names, page URLs, or raw search queries appear anywhere in this paper or its source notebooks. All identifiers are hashed (client_hash_id, content_hash_id).

04 · Methodology

Methodology

Target label — still_declining_may. The obvious candidate, is_declining (comparing early vs. late impressions within the same window), is circular: it's a deterministic function of two features already in the training data, so a model "predicting" it would just re-derive arithmetic. Instead, the label compares each page's May 2026 daily impression rate against its late-window rate — a genuinely future outcome the model never sees at training time.

Features: avg_position_90d, char_count, clicks_90d, competition_level, content_type, ctr, imp_early, imp_late, impressions_90d, main_intent, search_volume, word_count — all observed strictly within the feature window, or stable content metadata.

Baseline (rule-based): flag a page when impressions_90d ≥ 500 AND 0 < avg_position_90d ≤ 20 AND ctr < 0.4% AND is_declining; score = impressions_90d if flagged, else 0. 22,971 of 260,285 rows (8.8%) were flagged.

Validation — size-balanced GroupKFold. A plain GroupKFold risks badly unbalanced folds here: 41 clients with a mean of 6,348 rows but a standard deviation of 8,415 — smallest client has 11 rows, largest has 31,887. A greedy bin-packing assignment (each client goes to whichever fold currently holds the fewest rows) produced 5 folds of roughly 52,000 rows each, with no client split across folds — so no model can get credit for memorizing an account instead of learning a real pattern.

Leakage checks. Run twice: once on the raw candidate fields (confirming the snapshot-date trap above), and again on the final feature set actually used in the model — confirming no excluded column leaked in. A second check compared this honest split against a naive random 80/20 split that ignores client_hash_id entirely (see Results).

FINDING · FLOOR EFFECT

The tree's apparent win doesn't hold up. Permutation importance on the full-feature tree shows it relies almost entirely on one feature, imp_late (0.304 of ~0.31 total importance) — every content or ranking feature sits near zero. The label has a floor effect: when imp_late is already near zero, there's almost no room left to drop another 20% in relative terms, so the label lands on "still declining" almost by construction. Re-running without imp_early/imp_late drops Precision@50 from 0.940 to 0.760 — a real result, but a much smaller edge over the baseline (0.62) than the headline number suggested.

FINDING · SPLIT LEAKAGE

A second, independent check confirms the stakes of split design. Comparing this honest grouped split against a naive random 80/20 split (ignoring client_hash_id) shows the naive split overstates precision at every K — by as much as +0.12 at K=50 — even though every client technically appears in both train and test under the naive split. This mirrors a split-design flaw identified independently in the FlyRank research paper's own methodology.

06 · Limitations

Limitations & honest framing

Claim, rewritten to what the evidence actually supports: "Within this size-balanced client-grouped validation, the decision tree's precision@K was measured to be higher than the recomputed baseline rule at every K tested — but permutation importance showed this was driven almost entirely by a single feature tied mechanically to how the check-only label was constructed, not a broader content-quality signal. This is directional evidence a learned model can outperform the hand-set rule on this exact label, and decision-support for further investigation — not proof the tree learned something a human couldn't have written as a rule."

  • Client concentration. In every version of this ranking — baseline and model alike — a small number of clients dominate the top of the queue (2–4 of 41 clients take the majority of top-ranked rows, though not the same clients each time). A real handoff needs a per-client cap, not raw rank order.
  • Content-type coverage. The baseline's top-20 review was 100% keyword article. This queue hasn't been separately validated against other content types.
  • No causal claim. Nothing here shows that refreshing a flagged page causes it to recover — only whether decline continued into May 2026.
  • A snapshot in time. Trained on data through 2026-04-30; says nothing about what's true after that window without re-validation.
  • Not a ranking predictor. This system prioritizes review effort — it does not model or predict search-engine ranking behavior.
07 · Recommendations

Ranked recommendations

Who this is for: a content reviewer deciding which pages to look at first in a weekly refresh cycle — a prioritization aid, not an automated action.

BEFORE ACTING ON refresh_review
  • Check whether a SERP feature is likely absorbing clicks — avg_position_90d measures a ranking slot, not real visibility to a searcher.
  • Check whether a late-window collapse in impressions looks technical/indexing-related rather than content-related.
  • Check whether the page belongs to one of the clients currently dominating the queue, and confirm that client's own priorities first.
NEVER AUTOMATE
  • Auto-publishing or auto-editing content from the score alone — this is a review queue, not a content generator.
  • Treating the priority score as a calibrated confidence percentage — it's a rank, and it partly leans on a mechanical shortcut in the label (see Limitations).
MONITORING TRIGGERS
  • Content-type drift — re-validate if new content shifts away from keyword article.
  • Client-mix drift — rebuild fold assignment if the active-client population changes meaningfully.
  • Time decay — re-run the full pipeline once real data extends ~90 days past 2026-04-30.
